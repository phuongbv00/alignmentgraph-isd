\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Corporate training is a continuous activity. Programs for onboarding, role transitions, and upskilling are revised whenever technology, internal processes, business requirements, or post-deployment feedback change. Our experience building and operating such programs suggests that what designers lack is not a tool that produces better first drafts, but one that follows a program through its entire lifecycle---from a vaguely articulated need, through the first complete design, to the stream of adjustments that follows every deployment. The central challenge in that lifecycle is that \emph{design does not end at the first generation}: learner and instructor feedback, incidents in the field, and technology shifts continually produce refinement requests---add a learning objective, tighten a rubric, change the duration, switch the delivery format. Each request triggers a refinement round on the existing program, and these rounds recur across many working sessions over the program's lifetime.

Recent LLM-agent research on instructional systems design (ISD) does not address this challenge. Theory-grounded agents such as ReAct-ADDIE, ADDIE-Agent, Dick-Carey-Agent, and RPISD-Agent~\cite{isdagentbench,yao2023react,branch2009addie,dick1978systematic,tripp1990rapid}, as well as EduPlanner~\cite{zhang2025eduplanner}, Instructional Agents~\cite{yao2025instructionalagents}, and AgentLesson~\cite{agentlesson2026}, apply ISD theory and learning principles to maximize output quality within a \emph{single design pass}: a fixed brief goes in, and the best possible draft comes out. ISD-Agent-Bench~\cite{isdagentbench} likewise evaluates one design round per scenario and does not measure long-term iteration with a designer.

When a refinement request must be handled with these architectures, the designer faces a dilemma. The first option is \emph{full regeneration}: feed in the updated brief and rebuild the whole program. Its cost scales with the size of the program rather than the size of the change, and---more damaging to quality---it discards every part that had been reviewed, corrected, and validated in earlier rounds. The second option is \emph{uncontrolled targeted editing}: prompt the model with the previous design and the requested change. This is cheaper, but nothing guarantees that the edited part remains consistent with the rest: a newly added objective may be measured by no assessment, and a tightened rubric may drift away from the activities it grades. Such edits silently violate \emph{constructive alignment}~\cite{biggs1996enhancing}, the foundational principle that objectives, learning activities, and assessments must correspond. The root cause of the dilemma is structural: existing architectures do not represent the alignment relations among design components as explicit, traceable state. Without that state, the system cannot answer \emph{``what does this change affect?''}, and therefore cannot edit safely.

Three converging trends make this problem timely. Organizations already use LLMs to generate training programs at growing scale, so every program generated today is a future maintenance obligation. Alignment degradation is a cumulative, invisible defect---a broken program still ``looks fine'' section by section and only fails at deployment. And as base-model capability saturates on many benchmarks~\cite{deveci2025ouroboros,shukla2025scalingwall}, the quality of agentic systems depends increasingly on the \emph{harness} around the model, with the largest effects on small models~\cite{cho2026harness}---exactly as enterprises shift toward small language models (SLMs) for cost reasons~\cite{belcak2025slm}.

We address the problem with \textbf{AlignmentGraph-ISD}, an AI agent harness developed under a design-science methodology~\cite{hevner2004design} and built on the premise that an agent is the combination of a harness and a model. The harness represents the instructional design as an explicit, versioned \emph{alignment graph} and organizes every other mechanism around that state: a planner-driven multi-agent workflow that assigns each artifact to a specialist agent, a pedagogical verifier that detects alignment violations, \emph{cascade-scoped revision} that propagates any change to exactly the affected subgraph, multi-session memory that lets the design persist across sessions, conversational brief elicitation for the input side of the lifecycle, and pluggable agentic retrieval for organization-specific domain knowledge. Solving the core refinement problem alone would not make a practical tool, so the harness also covers the surrounding requirements: briefs that do not exist yet at project start (\cref{sec:elicitation}), domain conventions a generic agent cannot know (\cref{sec:agentic-rag}), and first-pass quality that must remain at least competitive with existing agents---designers will not adopt a tool that refines well but generates poorly.

We evaluate the harness along two research questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1} (first-pass quality): On ISD-Agent-Bench, how does AlignmentGraph-ISD compare with existing agents in artifact quality?
  \item \textbf{RQ2} (refinement efficiency): In multi-session refinement, does the alignment-graph mechanism localize edits at lower cost than full regeneration without degrading alignment, compared with both horns of the dilemma executed by the strongest baseline agent?
\end{itemize}

This paper makes two contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{AlignmentGraph-ISD}, a harness that represents design artifacts and their alignment relations (\textsl{measures}, \textsl{supports}, \textsl{evaluates}) as an explicit versioned graph and organizes verification, cascade-scoped revision, orchestration, memory, elicitation, and agentic retrieval around it, including a refinement entrypoint for multi-session refinement (\cref{sec:harness}).
  \item \textbf{Empirical evidence} on ISD-Agent-Bench under both an LLM and a 9B SLM, and a multi-session refinement experiment in the IT/software-engineering domain that operationalizes ``effective refinement'' as four process metrics---cascade scope, edit locality, cost per refinement round, and alignment preservation---measured under four cross-agent control conditions, with a case study demonstrating the full workflow including brief elicitation (\cref{sec:case-study,sec:experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In recent years, commercial large language models (LLMs) have progressed from
supporting individual writing tasks to handling nearly the full instructional
systems design (ISD) process: systems based on GPT-4 and comparable models can
generate lesson plans, assessment sets, and even complete course-material packages
from a single design request
~\cite{yao2025instructionalagents,zhang2025eduplanner,agentlesson2026,zeng2026coma,hu2024teachingplan}.
Several comparative studies have validated quality at the component level:
learning objectives comparable to faculty-authored versions~\cite{doyle2025objectives},
multiple-choice questions comparable to human-written items~\cite{doughty2024mcq},
lesson plans indistinguishable from colleagues' work under blind review
~\cite{cooper2026indistinguishable}, and classroom effectiveness that is not
inferior~\cite{karaman2024chatgptlesson}. With simulation--refinement loops,
quality can reach the level of experienced teachers~\cite{hu2025teachingsim}.
These tools have also entered instructional designers' practice
~\cite{yang2025idpractice,lessonplanner2024}.

Deploying these systems in educational institutions nevertheless remains difficult
because they depend on commercial APIs. A complete ISD workflow requires many model
calls and therefore accumulates substantial cost; higher-education surveys report
that financial constraints are slowing AI adoption~\cite{robert2025educause}.
Learner data must also pass through third-party infrastructure even though privacy
concerns remain inadequately addressed~\cite{yan2024ethical}. Many universities
have built internal gateways to control budgets and data~\cite{mithun2025aiverde},
and self-hosting open-weight models becomes cheaper than API use at sufficient
volume~\cite{pan2025onpremise}. Cost and data safety are thus documented barriers
to institutional deployment, motivating interest in open-weight small language
models (SLMs) that run on-premise: open LLM infrastructure for distance-learning
universities~\cite{zesch2024flexi}, evaluations of self-hosted models for schools
~\cite{chan2026bigger}, and locally run content-authoring frameworks motivated by
privacy and cost~\cite{reza2025smallmodels}.

With SLMs, the weaker foundation model makes output quality more dependent on the
surrounding harness---how it decomposes work, coordinates execution, and controls
quality. Small models remain weak at tool use, but decomposed multi-agent
frameworks narrow the gap with larger models~\cite{shen2024multillmagent}; agent
scaffolding helps 0.5B--3B models approach the quality of larger groups
~\cite{kang2025agentdistill}; and SLMs may suffice for most agentic workloads
~\cite{belcak2025slm,sharma2025slmagents}. Harness sensitivity is not monotonic
across model sizes~\cite{cho2026harness}, however, so the relationship among
quality, harness design, and model size must be measured rather than assumed.

This context reveals the central research gap: existing ISD and lesson-planning
agents are built and evaluated on capable commercial models, while the closest
studies each address only one part of the problem
~\cite{jaldi2026slmassessment,zheng2025lessonplanlm,reza2025smallmodels,lee2026educlaw,wei2026slmeducation}.
To our knowledge, no study has shown that an agent system can retain end-to-end ISD
quality on small models without fine-tuning, nor has any study evaluated that
quality systematically across a model-size ladder.

This gap also raises a measurement problem because both common evaluation tools
have limitations. LLM-judge rubric scoring---used by current ISD and education
benchmarks~\cite{isdagentbench,edubench2025,tutorbench2025,mathtutorbench2025}---
exhibits documented systematic biases
~\cite{zheng2023judging,panickssery2024llm,norman2026reliability}; here, the judge
model family also overlaps with the generator family. Human-expert evaluation is
more trustworthy but does not scale to a ladder with multiple sizes, runs, and
systems. More importantly, constructive alignment locates design quality in the
relationships among objectives, activities, and assessments
~\cite{biggs1996enhancing}: a design may read fluently and appear sound while
remaining seriously misaligned. Neither evaluation approach makes such alignment
signals explicit, and existing automated alignment methods score agreement with a
pre-authored standard or reference
~\cite{porter2002measuring,webb1997criteria,xu2025itemalignment,silva2026lessonbench},
which a wholly machine-generated design does not provide. Evaluating quality
retention therefore requires measures that read alignment signals from the
generated design itself; this study assembles such measures from established
criteria and reports them alongside the judge scores (\Cref{sec:metrics}).

This study aims to retain ISD quality as models shrink by using a multi-agent
harness with explicit alignment state (\Cref{sec:method}). We pursue this aim
through four objectives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Objective 1 (design).} Build a multi-agent ADDIE harness that
  keeps alignment state explicit throughout generation and exports it for audit.
  \item \textbf{Objective 2 (judge scale).} Compare the harness with six
  benchmark agents on ISD-Agent-Bench's LLM-judge scores at each size of the Qwen
  0.8B--9B ladder, and test whether the gap widens as the model shrinks.
  \item \textbf{Objective 3 (alignment signals).} Measure the same outputs on a
  panel of alignment signals computed without an LLM, and test the same size
  interaction.
  \item \textbf{Objective 4 (mechanism).} Attribute the observed behaviour to
  parts of the harness by disabling graph context and the verifier.
\end{itemize}

This paper contributes AlignmentGraph-ISD: a multi-agent harness that organizes
ADDIE program generation around an alignment graph---explicit alignment state that
is inspectable and auditable. At the end of each run, the complete graph is
exported so that an instructional designer can inspect each
objective--activity--assessment relation and intervene when needed, following the
teacher-in-the-loop principle.

Judge-based rubric scores alone cannot show whether the alignment relations hold
in a generated design. The evaluation therefore extends ISD-Agent-Bench with an
alignment-signal measurement that uses no LLM, defines every score as a fixed
function of text, and reports a panel of seven equally ranked signals from two
mechanically independent instrument families (\Cref{sec:metrics}). It
operationalizes established criteria
~\cite{biggs1996enhancing,porter2002measuring,webb1997criteria} rather than
proposing a new construct, and interpretation is limited to the signals it
measures.

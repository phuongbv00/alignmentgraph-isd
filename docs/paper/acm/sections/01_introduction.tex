\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In recent years, agent systems built on commercial large language models (LLMs)
have progressed from supporting individual writing tasks to handling nearly the
full instructional systems design (ISD) process: systems built on GPT-4 and
comparable models generate lesson plans, assessment sets, and even complete
course-material packages from a single design request
~\cite{yao2025instructionalagents,zhang2025eduplanner,agentlesson2026,zeng2026coma,hu2024teachingplan}.
Several comparative studies have validated quality at the component level:
learning objectives comparable to faculty-authored versions~\cite{doyle2025objectives},
multiple-choice questions comparable to human-written items~\cite{doughty2024mcq},
lesson plans indistinguishable from colleagues' work under blind review
~\cite{cooper2026indistinguishable}, and classroom effectiveness that is not
inferior~\cite{karaman2024chatgptlesson}. With simulation--refinement loops,
quality can reach the level of experienced teachers~\cite{hu2025teachingsim}.
These tools have also entered instructional designers' practice
~\cite{yang2025idpractice,lessonplanner2024}.

Deploying these systems in educational institutions nevertheless remains difficult
because they depend on commercial APIs. A complete ISD workflow requires many model
calls and therefore accumulates substantial cost; higher-education surveys report
that financial constraints are slowing AI adoption~\cite{robert2025educause}.
Learner data must also pass through third-party infrastructure even though privacy
concerns remain inadequately addressed~\cite{yan2024ethical}. Many universities
have built internal gateways to control budgets and data~\cite{mithun2025aiverde},
and self-hosting open-weight models becomes cheaper than API use at sufficient
volume~\cite{pan2025onpremise}. Cost and data safety are thus documented barriers
to institutional deployment, motivating interest in open-weight small language
models (SLMs) that run on-premise: open LLM infrastructure for distance-learning
universities~\cite{zesch2024flexi}, evaluations of self-hosted models for schools
~\cite{chan2026bigger}, and locally run content-authoring frameworks motivated by
privacy and cost~\cite{reza2025smallmodels}.

With SLMs, the weaker foundation model can carry less of the work on its own, so
end-to-end quality depends more on the system built around it---how it
decomposes the task into narrower calls, coordinates their execution, and
controls quality. Multi-agent decomposition is the documented route: small
models remain weak at tool use, but decomposed multi-agent frameworks narrow the
gap with larger models~\cite{shen2024multillmagent}; agent scaffolding helps
0.5B--3B models approach the quality of larger groups
~\cite{kang2025agentdistill}; and SLMs may suffice for most agentic workloads
~\cite{belcak2025slm,sharma2025slmagents}. The benefit is not monotonic
across model sizes~\cite{cho2026harness}, however, so the relationship among
quality, system design, and model size must be measured rather than assumed.

For ISD, what such a system must preserve is specific. Constructive alignment
locates design quality in the relationships among learning objectives,
teaching--learning activities, and assessments rather than in any artifact alone
~\cite{biggs1996enhancing}: a design may read fluently and appear sound while
remaining seriously misaligned. In existing agent systems this coherence is
implicit---carried in prose passed between steps and in the model's own
context---so it rests on the model's capacity to keep a long, multiply
constrained generation consistent, precisely the capacity expected to diminish
first as the model shrinks. Downscaling therefore puts alignment at risk before
fluency, and in a form that neither a reader nor a generic rubric readily sees.

This context reveals the central research gap, and it has two halves that meet in
the same place. Existing ISD and lesson-planning agents are built and evaluated on
capable commercial models
~\cite{yao2025instructionalagents,zhang2025eduplanner,agentlesson2026,hu2024teachingplan,isdagentbench},
and none of the surveyed systems represents the
objective--activity--assessment relations as explicit, checkable state during
generation---coherence is entrusted to the model (\Cref{sec:related-work}). The
closest studies each address only one part of the problem
~\cite{jaldi2026slmassessment,zheng2025lessonplanlm,reza2025smallmodels,lee2026educlaw,wei2026slmeducation}.
To our knowledge, no study has shown that an agent system can retain end-to-end ISD
quality on small models without fine-tuning, nor has any study evaluated that
quality systematically across a model-size ladder. The second half follows from
trying to answer the first: the question is about alignment, and the instrument
these benchmarks use---LLM judge rubric scoring---does not expose alignment
relations at all, so a design that reads well can score well while the relations
underneath it are broken. Answering the first half honestly therefore requires a
way to observe alignment signals that does not rest entirely on a judge model.

This study aims to retain ISD quality as models shrink by taking the coherence
burden off the model: instead of relying on the model to keep the design
consistent implicitly, the proposed multi-agent system (MAS),
AlignmentGraph-ISD, maintains the alignment relations as explicit shared state
that a model of any size can be checked against (\Cref{sec:method}). We pursue this aim through four objectives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Objective 1 (design).} Build a multi-agent ADDIE system that
  keeps alignment state explicit throughout generation and exports it for audit.
  \item \textbf{Objective 2 (judge scale).} Compare the MAS with six
  benchmark agents on ISD-Agent-Bench's LLM judge scores at each size of the Qwen
  0.8B--9B ladder, and test whether the gap widens as the model shrinks.
  \item \textbf{Objective 3 (alignment scores).} Measure the same outputs on a
  panel of alignment scores computed without an LLM, and test the same size
  interaction.
  \item \textbf{Objective 4 (mechanism).} Separate what the two design choices
  contribute, in a factorial experiment that crosses the decomposition of the
  work across agents with the substrate those agents share.
\end{itemize}

This paper contributes AlignmentGraph-ISD: a multi-agent system that organizes
ADDIE program generation around an alignment graph---explicit alignment state that
is inspectable and auditable. At the end of each run, the complete graph is
exported so that an instructional designer can inspect each
objective--activity--assessment relation and intervene when needed, following the
teacher-in-the-loop principle.

The second half of the gap shapes how the aim is evaluated. LLM judge
rubric scoring---the instrument of current ISD and education benchmarks
~\cite{isdagentbench,edubench2025,tutorbench2025,mathtutorbench2025}---exhibits
documented systematic biases~\cite{zheng2023judging,panickssery2024llm} and does
not expose the alignment relations themselves, while existing automated
alignment measures score agreement with a pre-authored standard
~\cite{porter2002measuring,webb1997criteria,xu2025itemalignment,silva2026lessonbench}
that a wholly generated design does not provide. The evaluation therefore
reports, alongside ISD-Agent-Bench's LLM judge scores, a panel of deterministic
alignment scores assembled from these established criteria; the measurement
setup is specified in \Cref{sec:metrics}.

\Cref{sec:discussion} discusses the findings and threats to validity; and
\Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.
